Current Market Leaders

The dominant players in blood coagulation monitoring today, all relying on invasive, intermittent blood testing. None offer continuous or non-invasive monitoring.

No DOAC support · No continuous monitoring

Clinical / Surgical Viscoelastic

The gold standard in viscoelastic coagulation testing for cardiac surgery, trauma, and liver transplant. Cartridge-based, requires fresh blood sample. Single test takes 10–30 min with no continuous output.

Blood draw required · Batch testing · 10–30 min gaps
Werfen (Instrumentation Lab)

Clinical / Surgical Viscoelastic

The primary European competitor to TEG. Rotational thromboelastometry platform widely adopted in EU hospitals for cardiac surgery and trauma. Same fundamental limitation: invasive, batch, not real-time.

Operator-dependent · Blood draw required · Not real-time
HemoSonics
Quantra Hemostasis Analyzer (QPlus / QStat)
Clinical / Surgical Sonorheometry

The newest FDA-cleared viscoelastic platform (QPlus cartridge, 2018 De Novo). The QStat cartridge won expanded 510(k) clearance for peripartum obstetric use in August 2025 and the system won a Silver 2026 Edison Award. Faster and more automated than TEG/ROTEM, but still a single-timepoint cartridge test.

Blood draw and cartridge required · Single timepoint, not continuous
Coalesenz, Inc.
iCoagLAB (laser speckle rheology)
Closest Competitor Optical

The nearest technology to KADI's: a Boston-based, MGH/Nadkarni-lab spinout using camera-based laser-speckle imaging of a blood drop to profile clot viscoelasticity in about 10 minutes from roughly 25µL of blood. NIH-funded (2024) specifically to extend the platform to mechanical circulatory support (ECMO, LVAD) monitoring.

Still requires a drawn sample and disposable cartridge per reading · By its own NIH-funded MCS program's stated aim, current reagents are not yet tailored to the wide heparin dose range typical in ECMO/MCS patients, an opening for a true in-line probe.
Siemens Healthineers
Xprecia Stride: POC Coagulation
Point-of-Care PT / INR / aPTT

Point-of-care coagulation analyzer used in clinics and hospital outpatient settings. Handheld, single-use strips. Faster than lab testing but still requires a blood draw and provides only a single snapshot result.

Blood draw required · Snapshot only · No home use
Diagnostica Stago
STA-R Max Coagulation Analyzer
Clinical Lab High-Volume

Leading laboratory coagulation analyzer used by hospital labs worldwide for high-volume PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, and anti-Xa testing. Stago commands a major share of the clinical lab coagulation market globally.

Lab-only · Hours turnaround · No bedside or home use
Where KADI Optics stands apart: Every competitor above, including Coalesenz, the nearest optical approach, shares the same fundamental constraint: a blood draw and a disposable cartridge are required, and every reading is a single timepoint. KADI's fiber probe sits in the bloodstream or the extracorporeal circuit itself and reports continuously, with zero consumables and zero blood loss, for as long as monitoring is needed.

Key Customer & Decision-Maker Segments

Cardiac Anesthesiologists Key decision-maker

The primary coagulation decision-makers in the OR, not the surgeon. Control heparin dosing and anticoagulation protocols. High influence on device adoption and hospital purchasing decisions.

Cardiac Surgeons

Primary end-user in the cardiac surgery beachhead. Benefit from real-time coagulation feedback during CPB and ECMO. Clinical champions for hospital purchasing decisions.

Perfusionists

Operate the CPB machine during cardiac surgery. Direct users of the KADI probe in the OR. Process-oriented; value workflow integration and ease of use.

Intensivists / ICU Physicians

Manage post-surgical patients on ECMO and anticoagulation therapy. High demand for continuous data; currently rely on intermittent blood draws every 1–2 hours.

DOAC Patients (Home)

~6M Americans on direct oral anticoagulants (rivaroxaban, apixaban, dabigatran). No home monitoring option exists today. RPM reimbursement via CPT codes 99454/99457 is a viable revenue stream.

Hematologists

Manage complex coagulopathies, bleeding disorders, and anticoagulation regimens. Potential clinical champions for hospital-wide adoption and research partnerships.

Pricing & Regulatory Benchmarks

The correct comparison class is the viscoelastic analyzer tier (TEG, ROTEM, Quantra), not simple single-endpoint POC devices like the Hemochron Signature Elite, which price in the low thousands and are not KADI's competitive set.

Device Pricing Benchmark

TEG/ROTEM/Quantra-class analyzers list at roughly $15,000–$100,000 depending on configuration. KADI's estimated $35,000–$45,000 target sits comfortably within that bracket, and below the capital equipment already budgeted for the surrounding ECMO stack: a Getinge Rotaflow console runs about $43,000 and a Maquet CardioHelp about $110,000.

Unit Economics Benchmark

At an estimated $5,000–$8,000 cost of goods (optical source, fiber, detector, housing) per unit at initial low volume, a $35,000–$45,000 sale price yields roughly 70% gross margin before service revenue. A $5,000–$7,000/year service and data contract, in line with typical 12–15% annual service pricing on comparable capital equipment, adds high-margin recurring revenue.

Regulatory Pathway In progress

Two established predicate product codes cover physical, non-chemical coagulation analyzers: JPA (TEG-family, mechanical/resonance-based, 21 CFR 864.5425) and QFR (Quantra-family “SEER Sonorheometry,” established via a 2018 De Novo, 21 CFR 864.5430). Neither monitors continuously, so FDA classification turning on measurement principle rather than frequency supports pursuing an established, precedented category rather than a novel one.

Competitive Intelligence From Our Own Interviews

Beyond desk research, direct industry contacts from the 59-interview I-Corps program (full log on the Customer Discovery page) confirmed how today's incumbents actually hold their ground.

Incumbents dominate by relationship, not just product

Shirlene Leongson (Helena Laboratories) named Siemens and Beckman Coulter as the entrenched defaults hospitals reach for; any new device has to be obviously better to justify the switching risk, echoed independently by Annette Abello (Werfen).

The DOAC shift is shrinking the home-testing market Roche built

Danielle Gioffre (Roche, National Account Manager, Point of Care) confirmed Roche holds roughly 85% of today's point-of-care coagulation segment, but that the segment itself is declining as warfarin gives way to generic DOACs that don't require routine home INR checks.

A new device's numbers must reconcile with the gold standard

Anis H. Khimani (Revvity) flagged the most common failure mode for new coagulation devices: readings that don't match established techniques erode trust before adoption even starts. Head-to-head validation against ACT/TEG is a precondition, not an afterthought.

Preanalytical sample quality is a real differentiator

Molly Mullen (Siemens) and Paul Panganiban (hospital lab) both flagged sample handling, hemolysis, icterus, and lipemia as concrete lab-operations pain points; KADI's zero-sample, in-line approach sidesteps this class of error entirely.
